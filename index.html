How it flies

          
        

        
Nothing here is a drawn curve.

        
240 times a second the plane is pushed by three forces and turned by one. Where it goes is whatever they add up to.


        
        

          
Lift
pushes at right angles to the air rushing past. Double the speed and it is four times as strong.

          
Drag
pulls straight back, and steals the speed that lift is made from.

          
Weight
pulls straight down, always.

          
The nose
is heavy, so the plane keeps turning to meet the air at about 5 degrees. Throw it hard and it has more lift than it needs: it climbs, slows, dips and climbs again. That slow swoop is real, and has a name: a phugoid.

        


        
        
Tilt the wing further into the air and it lifts more, up to about 13 degrees. Past that the air lets go of the paper. Lift falls away, drag jumps, and the plane drops until it is moving fast enough to fly again. That is a stall, and it is why a throw aimed at the ceiling lands at your feet.

        
Holding the screen lifts the nose a few degrees: slower, and a little less sink. Good inside rising air, costly into a headwind, and a stall if you ask for it with no speed left.


        
The air moves too

        
The wing only feels the air it is in. Wind carries it along or holds it back, in gusts. Warm roofs and sunny fields send up columns of rising air; cold water pulls air down. Wind blown against a hill has to climb, and takes the plane up with it. Just above the ground a cushion of air cuts the drag, which is why a low plane seems to float the last few metres.
